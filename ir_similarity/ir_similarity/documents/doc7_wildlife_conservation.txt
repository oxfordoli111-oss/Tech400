On a quiet island in Hawai'i, a small, bright orange native insect may soon return home, thanks to an innovative partnership and a first-of-its-kind conservation benefit agreement between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the State of Hawai'i, and the local Pūlama Lāna'i land management company. Conservation Benefit Agreements are voluntary agreements that provide incentives for non-Federal property owners to work with the Service to conserve species. In 2025, the Service worked with partners to complete the first such agreement in the United States, focused on reintroducing the orangeblack Hawaiian damselfly on Lāna'i, a small and mostly privately-owned Hawaiian island west of Maui. The orangeblack Hawaiian damselfly is found only in the Hawaiian Islands and was once the most abundant damselfly species in Hawai'i; it is now limited to 34 known populations and is no longer found at all on Kaua'i or Lāna'i. It was listed as endangered in 2016. One of the greatest threats to the species includes wetland and stream habitat loss, as well as non-native plants, agriculture, urban development, invasive fish, and habitat destruction by feral pigs.
